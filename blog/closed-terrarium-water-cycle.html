The Water Cycle Inside a Closed Terrarium

Understanding the self-sustaining ecosystem

By Aayush Lilani · 1 May 2026 · 6 min read

How does a closed terrarium water itself? Through a self-sustaining water cycle where plants release moisture through transpiration, water condenses on the glass walls, and droplets return to the soil. This cycle can continue indefinitely without adding water.

A closed terrarium is one of nature's most elegant demonstrations of a miniature ecosystem. Once sealed, it becomes a self-contained world where the same water molecules cycle endlessly between soil, plant, air, and glass. Understanding this process helps you create and maintain terrariums that thrive for years—even decades—without intervention.

The Science Behind Terrarium Transpiration

Plants absorb water through their roots and release it as vapor through tiny pores called stomata on their leaves. This process, called transpiration, is how plants regulate temperature and transport nutrients. In an open environment, this moisture escapes into the atmosphere. In a closed terrarium, it has nowhere to go.

The released water vapor rises and meets the cooler glass walls of the terrarium. Temperature differences between the interior air and the glass surface cause the vapor to condense into droplets—the same process that creates morning dew. These droplets eventually roll down the glass and return to the soil, completing the cycle.

Why Closed Terrariums Rarely Need Water

Once you establish the right moisture balance, a closed terrarium maintains itself. The key is starting with the correct amount of water—enough to sustain the cycle, but not so much that the system becomes waterlogged.

Key Insight

The world's oldest known closed terrarium was planted by David Latimer in 1960. He last watered it in 1972. It continues to thrive over 50 years later, demonstrating the remarkable stability of a well-balanced terrarium ecosystem.

Signs your water cycle is healthy:

  • Light condensation appears on the glass in the morning and clears by afternoon
  • Soil feels moist but not soggy when touched
  • Plants maintain vibrant green color without wilting
  • No standing water pools at the bottom of the container

When the Cycle Goes Wrong

Two problems can disrupt the water cycle: too much moisture or too little. Each has distinct symptoms and solutions.

Too much water creates constant heavy condensation, foggy glass you cannot see through, and eventually mold growth. The solution is simple: remove the lid for a few hours daily until the excess moisture evaporates. This may take several days for severely overwatered terrariums.

Too little water shows as absent condensation, dry soil, and wilting plants. Add water sparingly—a tablespoon at a time—and wait 24 hours to assess before adding more. It's easier to add water than to remove it.

Optimizing Your Terrarium's Microclimate

Light and temperature directly affect the water cycle's speed. More light increases transpiration; warmer temperatures speed evaporation. Finding the right balance depends on your plants and environment.

Placement recommendations:

  • Bright indirect light: Near a north-facing window or several feet from east/west windows
  • Avoid direct sunlight: Glass magnifies heat, potentially cooking plants and disrupting the water balance
  • Stable temperature: Keep away from air conditioning vents and heating sources

"A terrarium is a conversation between water, light, and patience. Get the first two right, and the third becomes easy."

The Role of Different Plants

Not all plants transpire at the same rate. Ferns and moss release more moisture than succulents (which shouldn't be in closed terrariums anyway). When selecting plants, consider how their water needs will interact.

For beginners, we recommend starting with:

  • Fittonia (nerve plant): High transpiration, dramatic when thirsty, quick recovery
  • Sheet moss: Excellent moisture indicator—browns when dry, greens when watered
  • Selaginella: Native to India, adapts well to terrarium humidity

Long-Term Maintenance

A properly balanced closed terrarium needs almost no maintenance. Check it weekly for the first month, then monthly after that. You're looking for the same healthy signs: morning condensation, moist soil, vibrant plants.

Annual maintenance might include:

  • Pruning plants that outgrow the container
  • Removing any dead leaves (which can cause mold)
  • Wiping interior glass if mineral deposits accumulate

The water cycle does the rest. Once established, your terrarium becomes a living demonstration of nature's efficiency—a complete ecosystem contained in glass, running on sunlight and recycled rain.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do closed terrariums need watering?

Closed terrariums rarely need watering after initial setup. The sealed environment recycles water through evaporation and condensation. Add water only if condensation stops appearing.

How long can a closed terrarium survive without opening?

A properly balanced closed terrarium can survive for years, even decades. The oldest known closed terrarium has thrived since 1960 with minimal intervention.
