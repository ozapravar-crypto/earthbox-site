The Right Soil for Terrariums

Building the foundation layer

By Aayush Lilani · 27 April 2026 · 6 min read

The substrate in your terrarium isn't just dirt—it's the foundation of an entire ecosystem. It anchors roots, stores moisture, hosts beneficial microbes, and determines drainage. Get it wrong, and you'll fight persistent problems. Get it right, and the system largely takes care of itself. This guide explains what makes terrarium substrate different from ordinary potting soil and how to create the perfect mix.

Why Regular Soil Doesn't Work

Garden soil and standard potting mix will kill most terrariums within months. Here's why:

Compaction. Regular soil compresses over time, eliminating air pockets roots need. In the confined terrarium environment, this happens faster than in open pots.

Water retention. Standard potting mixes are formulated for plants that get watered regularly and drain freely. Terrariums hold water indefinitely. A mix that works in a pot becomes waterlogged swamp in a sealed environment.

Pathogens. Garden soil carries bacteria, fungi, and pest eggs that might be harmless outdoors but become devastating in the humid, enclosed terrarium environment. One fungal spore can colonise an entire system.

The Closed System Difference

Outdoor gardens flush pathogens and excess moisture naturally. Terrariums trap everything. What goes in, stays in—including problems.

The Layer System

Proper terrarium substrate isn't a single mixture—it's a series of layers working together:

Layer 1: Drainage (2-3 cm)

The bottom layer creates space for excess water to collect without saturating roots. Common materials:

  • Leca (expanded clay balls): Lightweight, pH-neutral, won't decompose. Our preferred choice.
  • Gravel or pebbles: Works but adds weight. Choose smooth stones without sharp edges.
  • Activated charcoal pieces: Can serve as drainage while also filtering.

Layer 2: Separation Barrier

Without a barrier, soil particles migrate into the drainage layer, eventually clogging it. Options:

  • Sphagnum moss: Natural, attractive where visible, slightly acidic (good for most terrarium plants).
  • Mesh screen: Synthetic but permanent—won't decompose over time.
  • Landscape fabric: Practical but less attractive if visible.

We use a thin layer of dried sphagnum moss, which also adds a buffer of moisture retention above the drainage.

Layer 3: Activated Charcoal (1 cm)

Charcoal isn't strictly necessary, but it significantly improves long-term terrarium health:

  • Absorbs odours from decomposing organic matter
  • Filters impurities from circulating water
  • Inhibits bacterial growth through natural antimicrobial properties
  • Provides trace minerals as it slowly breaks down

Charcoal Source

Use horticultural activated charcoal, not BBQ briquettes. Briquettes contain additives toxic to plants. Aquarium charcoal works well and is readily available in India.

Layer 4: Growing Medium (5-8 cm)

The main substrate layer where roots live. This requires careful formulation.

The Ideal Growing Mix

Terrarium substrate needs three properties in balance:

Moisture retention sufficient to maintain humidity without watering, but not so much that roots stay waterlogged.

Aeration for root respiration and beneficial microbial activity. Compacted, airless soil kills roots.

Stability to maintain structure over years without breaking down into sludge.

Our standard mix:

  • 2 parts coco coir (moisture retention, structure)
  • 1 part perlite (aeration, drainage)
  • 1 part orchid bark, finely chopped (structure, slow decomposition)
  • ½ part worm castings (gentle nutrients, beneficial microbes)
"Mix should feel like a wrung-out sponge when squeezed—moist but not dripping. If water runs out, it's too wet. If it crumbles apart, too dry."

Component Deep Dive

Coco coir is our preferred base over peat moss. It's more sustainable (made from coconut husks), less acidic, and resists compaction better. It holds moisture without becoming waterlogged and provides excellent root anchoring.

Perlite—those white volcanic glass pieces—creates permanent air pockets. Unlike organic materials, it doesn't decompose or compress. Use horticultural grade, not construction perlite.

Orchid bark adds structure and decomposes extremely slowly. It creates channels for water and air movement. We chop it to 5-10mm pieces for terrariums; larger chunks suit bigger containers.

Worm castings provide gentle, slow-release nutrition and introduce beneficial soil microbes. These microorganisms are crucial for healthy, self-sustaining terrariums. Don't substitute chemical fertiliser—it's too strong and disrupts the biological balance.

Where to Source in India

Coco coir: any garden centre. Perlite: garden centres or Amazon. Orchid bark: speciality nurseries or online. Worm castings: vermicompost sellers or make your own.

Alternative Mixes for Specific Plants

Some terrarium plants have specialised needs:

Moss-dominant terrariums: Reduce or eliminate bark, increase coir ratio. Moss needs consistently moist conditions and doesn't rely on deep substrate.

Fern terrariums: Add extra perlite and charcoal. Fern roots need exceptional aeration and are sensitive to stagnant conditions.

Carnivorous plant terrariums: Pure sphagnum moss or peat with perlite only. These plants require nutrient-free, acidic substrate. Never add worm castings or fertiliser.

Sterilisation: Essential or Optional?

Opinions vary on substrate sterilisation. Our position:

For closed terrariums: highly recommended. Killing off pathogens before sealing prevents problems you'll struggle to fix later. Methods:

  • Oven: Bake at 100°C for 30 minutes. Kills most pathogens, some beneficial organisms.
  • Microwave: Moisten substrate, microwave 90 seconds per 500g. Fast but less thorough.
  • Boiling water: Pour over substrate, drain, and dry. Effective and equipment-free.

After sterilisation, reintroduce beneficial microbes via worm castings or a sprinkle of healthy terrarium soil from an established system.

Troubleshooting Substrate Problems

Soil staying wet weeks after setup: Mix too dense. Remove and remix with more perlite, or start over with better drainage.

White fuzzy mould on surface: Often harmless soil fungus consuming organic matter. Increase ventilation temporarily. If persistent, reduce moisture and add more charcoal.

Soil compacting over time: Normal in systems over 2 years old. Gently aerate with a chopstick, working around roots. Consider top-dressing with fresh mix.

Unpleasant smell: Anaerobic (oxygen-deprived) conditions from poor drainage or over-watering. Open the terrarium, improve drainage, reduce water. Charcoal helps absorb odours.

Substrate is infrastructure. It's not glamorous, but getting it right makes everything else easier. Invest time in proper layering and mixing at setup—your terrarium will reward you for years.

Frequently Asked Questions

Do I need charcoal in terrarium soil?

Charcoal is not essential but helps absorb odors and toxins in closed terrariums. Add a thin layer above drainage rocks.

Can I use regular potting soil for terrariums?

Yes, but amend it. Mix with perlite and bark for better drainage. Pure potting soil retains too much moisture for closed terrariums.
